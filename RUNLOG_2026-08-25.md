## 1. Hydraulic rig — the governance layer, off the bearing

**Dataset.** ZeMA hydraulic test rig via UCI. 2205 cycles of 60 s each,
seventeen channels, four components degraded independently with graded severity.
Six pressures and motor power at 100 Hz, two flows at 10 Hz, four temperatures
and a vibration channel at 1 Hz.

**Why it is the right analogue.** Repeated fixed-duration heavily-loaded cycles;
pressure, temperature and motor power — the sensors AAG already has; several
failure modes present at once; graded severity rather than present/absent; and
the rig flags the cycles where it is not confident conditions had settled.

**What does NOT carry over, and this is the point.** There is no bearing here,
no geometry, no order to predict. The physics library contributes nothing. What
was lifted across is the governance layer alone: per-regime robust baselines,
freezing, sequential evidence with boundaries derived from a stated false-alarm
rate, and abstention. If that layer only worked bolted to bearing code, this run
would have failed. It did not, and it also did not succeed everywhere.

**Code written for this.** `src/cbmx/io/hydraulic.py`,
`src/cbmx/physics/hydraulic.py`, `src/cbmx/health/sequential.py`,
`tools/eval_hydraulic.py`.

### Discipline actually enforced in the code

- **Symptoms declared before scoring**, from first-principles fluid power, each
  with a declared direction of fault. Eight symptoms, two per component. None
  was selected by how well it separated the labels.
- **CE, CP and SE are never read.** The rig computes these "virtual" channels
  from the temperature channels. Using them to detect a cooler fault scores
  against a quantity derived from the thing being detected. Published
  near-perfect cooler results on this dataset should be read with that in mind.
- **Channel roles resolved at commissioning** from cycles 0–199 with no label
  read: working line = highest mean pressure (PS1), switched line = largest
  within-cycle swing (PS2), hot/cold temperature probes = TS2/TS4.
- **Per-component healthy pools.** Only 21 of 2205 cycles are optimal on all
  four components and only 10 of those are settled — and they are contiguous.
  Ten adjacent cycles is not a baseline. Pools are 180–245 fit cycles each.
- **Oil temperature is the regime variable** for valve, pump and accumulator
  (3 °C bins, 8 regimes over 36.2–58.0 °C). Deliberately **not** for the cooler,
  where temperature is downstream of the fault and binning on it would condition
  on the outcome.

### Results — parity split, freeze on

α = 0.02, β = 0.10 → Wald boundaries +3.81 / −2.28 nats.

| Component | held-out healthy: named | detection (per cycle) | evidence/cycle → cycles to decide | fault episodes named |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cooler | **0.0 %** (n=244) | 99.6 % at both severities | 1.12–1.14 → **3 cycles** | 2 / 2 |
| Valve | 23.9 % (n=184) | 70 % / 73 % / 48 % | 0.08 / 0.62 / 0.80 → 46 / 6 / 5 | 79 / 108 |
| Pump | 0.8 % (n=244) | 31 % / 25 % | 0.10 / 0.38 → 40 / 10 | 12 / 24 |
| Accumulator | 0.0 % (n=180) | 0 % / 0 % / 35 % | −0.34 / −0.23 / 0.13 → never / never / 30 | 3 / 9 |

### Reading these honestly

**The cooler number is real but it is the easy case.** The temperature drop
across the cooler is close to a direct measurement of the failure mode. The
published literature agrees the cooler is an easy target. Do not lead with it.

**The valve's 23.9 % is not a false-alarm rate, it is a time-constant
mismatch — and it should be reported as one, not quietly reframed.** The rig
switches the valve condition **every 10 cycles**. The sequential test needs 5 to
46 cycles of evidence by its own Wald estimate. Held-out healthy cycles that
immediately follow a fault block inherit an accumulator that has not yet decayed.
A test whose entire purpose is to require persistent evidence cannot be scored
fairly against a condition that flips faster than its decision time. On AAG the
condition does not change every ten arrestments. The episode-level numbers and
the evidence-rate column are the ones that transfer.

**The accumulator is a miss and should be stated as one.** Ripple and stiffness
do not move at 115 bar or 100 bar — the evidence is *negative* there, meaning the
symptoms actively point away from a fault. Only near-total failure (90 bar) is
picked up, at 35 %. Our accumulator symptoms are insensitive until the component
is nearly gone. The literature also reports the accumulator as the hard target;
that is corroboration, not an excuse.

**The pump lands exactly on the edge, and the arithmetic is the interesting
part.** Weak leakage yields 0.10 nats of evidence per cycle, so Wald says about
40 cycles to a decision. The rig gives episodes of 41 cycles. The method needs
almost exactly as many cycles as the experiment provides — which is why 12 of 24
episodes are caught rather than 0 or 24. That is a quantitative statement about
required observation time, obtained without tuning anything.

### The freeze ablation — the strongest single result here

Re-run with `--no-freeze`, letting baselines keep learning through a developing
fault, which is what a self-tuning condition monitor does by default:

| Component | fault episodes named, freeze ON | freeze OFF |
|---|---|---|
| Cooler | 2 / 2 | 1 / 2 |
| Valve | 79 / 108 | **0 / 108** |
| Pump | 12 / 24 | **0 / 24** |
| Accumulator | 3 / 9 | **0 / 9** |

With freezing off the system reports **nothing, ever**, on three of four
components. The baseline absorbs the degradation as the new normal, the machine
gets worse, and the monitor stays quiet. It does not error. It does not degrade
gracefully. It says everything is fine, for ever. This is the silent failure mode
of every self-tuning condition monitor, and it is one line of mechanism.

### The time-split run — abstention under adversarial conditions

`--time-split` fits on the first 60 % of each healthy pool by cycle index, so the
later, hotter operating regimes are genuinely unseen:

| Component | held-out healthy: named | abstained (no baseline) |
|---|---|---|
| Cooler | 0.0 % | 0.0 % |
| Valve | **0.0 %** | **87.2 %** |
| Pump | **0.0 %** | **86.2 %** |
| Accumulator | **0.0 %** | **83.3 %** |

Given operating points it has never seen healthy, the system says *"I have no
baseline for this"* on 83–87 % of cycles and raises **zero** false alarms. It
does not extrapolate. Detection falls accordingly — valve 32 %, accumulator 0 %.
That trade is the product: it declines to answer rather than guessing, and it
says which cycles it declined on.

## 2. Paderborn — vibration works on real damage, current does not

**Code written for this.** `src/cbmx/physics/mcsa.py`, `tools/eval_mcsa.py`.
Bearing geometry in `physics/bearing.py` replaced with the dataset paper's own
published dimensions.

### The headline, from the full envelope pipeline

`tools/eval_dataset.py --dataset paderborn`, vibration, real accelerated-lifetime
damage, no training, no labels, geometry only:

| | result |
|---|---|
| Outer race (KA04, fatigue pitting, extent level 1) | **25 / 25 named correctly** |
| Inner race (KI21, fatigue pitting, extent level 1) | **0 / 15 — missed** |
| Wrong-part calls | **0** |
| False alarms on healthy records | **0 / 80** |

Commissioned band 500–2000 Hz, frozen, commissioned on two records which were
then excluded from the test.

**The outer-race result is the one to lead with.** Twenty-five for twenty-five on
damage that came from running a bearing to failure, not from a machined slot;
zero false alarms across eighty healthy records; and — the number a reviewer
will actually look for — zero cases where it named the wrong part. The
distinction matters: a classifier that must answer something will name the
quietest available part on a healthy machine. This one stayed silent eighty
times out of eighty.

**The inner-race result is a miss and is reported as one.** An inner-race defect
rotates through the load zone, so its energy spreads into sidebands about BPFI
rather than concentrating on it, and KI21 is extent level 1 — the mildest grade
the dataset defines. Both the raw-spectrum comparison and the full envelope
pipeline missed it. The dataset paper reports the same asymmetry, and notes
inner-ring damage "is not as easy to detect as outer ring damage in most
situations, because the signal is disguised by a modulation". Named cause,
named next step: widen the sideband model around BPFI and re-test on KI16 and
KI18, which are extent 3 and 2 respectively. Until that runs, the honest claim
is outer race only.

### Motor current: the answer is no

Same bearings, same files, same geometry, same evidence test — read off the
current channel instead of the accelerometer:

| channel | outer race, robust z vs healthy | detections |
|---|---|---|
| vibration | **+12.8 to +16.0** across all four operating conditions | 25 / 25 |
| motor current | **−0.5 to +0.2** | 0 / 40 |

Direct sideband MCSA does not detect these bearing faults. It also raised no
false alarms — it simply has nothing to say.

**This changes the ladder, and for the better.** The "works day one on installed
sensors" rung cannot be built on direct sideband detection of bearing faults in
current. The dataset paper reached 93.3 % from motor current, but only with
wavelet-packet features and a trained ensemble — and its own Table 9 shows that
when those models are trained on artificial damage and tested on real damage,
motor current drops to 60.9 % against 75.0 % for vibration. AAG will not have a
labelled corpus of real arresting-gear bearing failures to train on.

So the honest thing to tell Matthew Marko is: current will monitor the drive
itself, and it is the right place to start because it is already wired — but
bearing fault detection needs the vibration sensors he already said we may
reasonably propose. That makes his own invitation the load-bearing element of
the proposal rather than a nice-to-have, and it is a position that survives a
reviewer who knows drives.

### A silent bug found, and worth the space

The first Paderborn run reported **0 detections and 0 false alarms across 122
records** — which reads like an admirably conservative monitor. It was not. The
decimation helper `align_rate` rebuilt each `Record` positionally and stopped
short of four fields, so every record silently reverted to the default bearing
geometry: the CWRU nine-ball SKF 6205 instead of the eight-ball FAG 6203 in the
Paderborn rig. Outer race was being searched at 3.5848 × shaft instead of
3.0543. Every fault line landed on empty spectrum.

Two things follow. First, **a monitor that never speaks passes a false-alarm
test perfectly**, so the detection column has to be read beside it — always.
Second, the same bug was deleting `rpm_series`, which is what the variable-speed
and order-tracking work depends on; it would have made the Ottawa transient
result quietly untestable. Both are fixed, the rebuild is now field-preserving
by construction, and the reasoning is written into the code rather than into a
commit message.

### Geometry, corrected against the dataset's own paper

The catalogue entry for the Paderborn bearing had been standing in SKF 6203
dimensions. The dataset paper (Lessmeier, Kimotho, Zimmer & Sextro, PHM Society
European Conference 2016, Table 1) publishes the actual bearing: FAG 6203, pitch
circle 28.55 mm, rolling element 6.75 mm, 8 elements, 0°. The stand-in was 0.1 %
out on the raceway orders and 0.14 % out on the ball-defect order — small enough
to have stayed invisible for ever. Now cited.

### What was verified earlier in the day, and still holds

A bearing defect displaces the rotor at the geometry-predicted frequency, that
displacement modulates air-gap permeance, and the result appears in the stator
current as sidebands at |f₁ ± k·f_char| (Schoen et al., 1995). Same four caliper
dimensions, same orders, read off the wires instead of an accelerometer.

On the healthy K001 bearing, 80 files across all four operating conditions:

| Condition | n | shaft Hz | measured f₁ | outer race | inner race | ball | cage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| N09_M07_F10 | 20 | 15.00 | 59.99 | 4.4 ± 1.6 dB | 3.3 ± 2.2 | blocked | 4.6 ± 1.3 |
| N15_M01_F10 | 20 | 24.99 | 99.99 | 4.3 ± 1.5 | 4.0 ± 1.5 | blocked | 3.6 ± 1.0 |
| N15_M07_F04 | 20 | 24.99 | 99.98 | 4.7 ± 1.3 | 4.5 ± 1.2 | blocked | 3.1 ± 1.0 |
| N15_M07_F10 | 20 | 24.99 | 99.97 | 4.0 ± 1.5 | 4.8 ± 2.1 | blocked | 4.0 ± 1.0 |

Three things worth having:

**The supply frequency was measured, not assumed, and it checks out at two
independent speeds.** f₁ / shaft = 3.999 at 900 rpm and 4.000 at 1500 rpm. The
drive has four pole pairs. That was recovered from the data, and its consistency
across two speeds is evidence the measurement chain is right.

**A trap was found and closed.** The ball-defect order for this 6203 is 3.9877 —
within 0.3 % of the pole-pair count. So the ball-defect sideband lands within one
resolution cell of the second supply harmonic at *every* speed the rig offers.
Measured naively it reads **29 dB of ball-defect evidence on a certified-healthy
bearing**. The code now excludes lines that collide with k·f₁ and returns *not
observable* rather than a number. This is a physical impossibility result, not a
tuning choice: on this motor, at these speeds, a ball defect cannot be seen in
the current, and the correct output is to say so.

**Healthy prominence is 3–5 dB with a 1–2 dB spread, stable across all four
operating conditions.** That is a usable baseline. Whether a real spall clears it
is the open question — and it is open, because no fault data is staged.

One further point, now confirmed by the paper rather than only by our code: the
paper states that **damage at the rolling elements was never observed** in the
lifetime tests. There is no ball-defect record anywhere in the dataset, so the
blocked ball line costs nothing — but the guard stays, because on AAG the same
collision arithmetic will apply to whatever pole-pair count that drive has.

### Coverage caveat, stated plainly

Three bearings of twenty-six were transferred: K001 (healthy, 80 files), KA04
(real outer race, 26 files) and KI21 (real inner race, 16 files). The dataset
holds five real outer-race and six real inner-race bearings. The outer-race
result rests on one damaged bearing across four operating conditions. It should
be repeated on KA15, KA16, KA22 and KA30 before the number is quoted as a rate.

## 4. Motor current, second attempt — yes, with a rule

Asked whether current could be made to work, the answer turned out to be yes,
and finding it required tracing the signal path rather than trying more
transforms.

### What was tried and failed first

Four demodulation routes, all declared before scoring and all reported:

| method | outer race, robust z vs healthy |
|---|---|
| raw sideband prominence at f₁ ± k·BPFO | +0.7 |
| AM envelope demodulation about f₁ | +0.6 |
| Extended Park's Vector (three-phase modulus) | +0.4 |
| the same, Welch-averaged | +0.4 |

The two current channels were first verified to be a genuine three-phase pair —
120.01° apart, amplitudes matched to 0.02 % — so the third phase could be
reconstructed and Park's vector formed properly. It made no difference.

### Why, measured rather than guessed

Following the same fault through every channel the rig records, at the fault
order:

| channel | healthy → KA04 |
|---|---|
| accelerometer | **+26.5 dB** |
| radial force sensor | **+23.8 dB** |
| **shaft torque** | **−0.2 dB** ← it dies here |
| shaft speed | −1.0 dB |
| motor phase current | +1.8 dB (inside the healthy spread) |

An outer-race defect is a **radial** event: the rolling element strikes a fixed
point on the outer ring, perpendicular to rotation. It produces essentially no
torque ripple. Motor current responds to torque. No transform recovers what was
never transmitted — which is why Park's vector, envelope demodulation and raw
sidebands all return the same nothing.

This also corrects an error in our own module docstring. The air-gap permeance
mechanism (Schoen et al. 1995) applies to bearings that carry the **motor's own
rotor**. Paderborn's test bearings sit in a separate module downstream — as do
the bearings that matter on an arresting engine. For those, torque is the only
path.

### Where the signal does get through

Tracing every harmonic of BPFO instead of only the first two, at two speeds:

| speed | strongest torque response | frequency |
|---|---|---|
| 900 rpm | **5th** harmonic | 229.0 Hz |
| 1500 rpm | **3rd** harmonic | 229.0 Hz |

Different harmonic numbers, the same frequency — the signature of a structural
resonance rather than an order effect. And in the current sidebands, which is
the channel that matters:

| speed | k | healthy | damaged | contrast |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 900 rpm | 5 (229.0 Hz) | 3.0 dB | 10.1 dB | **+7.1** |
| 1500 rpm | 3 (229.0 Hz) | 4.2 dB | 11.5 dB | **+7.4** |

Every other harmonic is within ±2 dB. **The current carries the fault only
through a narrow torsional resonance at 229 Hz** — and the first and second
harmonics of BPFO, which is where the entire MCSA literature looks, are exactly
where the drive train and the speed controller suppress it.

### The detector, and its held-out result

Commission the band once on current (not on torque — an arresting engine has
current transducers and no shaft torque transducer, so a procedure needing one
cannot be run on the customer's machine), freeze it, then read whichever
harmonic of the geometry-derived order falls inside it. The orders still come
from four caliper dimensions; only the harmonic index depends on speed, and the
band does not.

Commissioning rule: a frequency qualifies only if the damaged-minus-healthy
contrast is present at **every** speed, scored by the **worst** of them. On this
rig that rule discards a +5.7 dB artefact at 503.7 Hz that appears at 900 rpm
and vanishes at 1500 rpm, and keeps 229 Hz. Band commissioned at **199–259 Hz**
on twelve records, all excluded from the test.

| | held-out result |
|---|---|
| Healthy K001, 74 records, four conditions | **0 false alarms** |
| KA04 @ 1500 rpm, 0.7 Nm, 1000 N | z = **+3.2** → 16 captures to a decision |
| KA04 @ 900 rpm, 0.7 Nm, 1000 N | z = **+2.1** → 108 captures |
| KA04 @ 1500 rpm, 0.7 Nm, **400 N** | z = +0.3 → nothing |
| KI21 (inner race) on the outer-race line | correctly quiet |

So current works, at a stated price: **about sixteen four-second captures at
rated speed and full load**, against vibration naming the same fault from a
single record, 25 times out of 25. And it needs the load — at 400 N radial the
signature disappears, which is physically what you would expect and is good news
for AAG, where an arrestment is the highest-load event the machine sees.

### Three limitations that must travel with this number

1. **One damaged bearing.** KA04 only. The band-finding rule was chosen after
   looking at the contrast curves on that bearing. The held-out records and the
   zero false alarms are real, but the rule itself is not yet validated —
   KA15, KA16, KA22 and KA30 are what would validate it.
2. **229 Hz is this rig's resonance.** On AAG the equivalent would have to be
   found at commissioning. That is a concrete, cheap Phase I task, and it is
   exactly what the historical test-site vibration data could be used for.
3. **Outer race only.** KI21's inner-race damage does not appear in any channel,
   vibration included, so nothing about inner-race detection in current has been
   tested either way.

### What this does to the ladder

| rung | sensor | what it detects | evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Day one, no hardware | existing motor current | outer-race degradation, at load, given a commissioned band | z = +3.2, 0 false alarms, ~16 captures |
| Proposed addition | vibration | the same fault from a single 4 s record | 25/25, 0 false alarms, 0 wrong-part calls |

The concession stays, and it is now quantitative rather than rhetorical: current
buys coverage on day one and needs roughly sixteen times as much observation to
say the same thing. That is the argument for the sensors Mr Marko invited, and
it is made in numbers a reviewer can check.

## 5. The speed transient — Shevach's question, answered

Glenn Shevach's point on the 24 August call is the one that decides whether any of
this is useful on an arresting engine:

> "you might not get certain fault modes excited... during a retract versus an
> arrestment" · "it's just a ton more energy during an arrestment that is getting
> absorbed"

The interesting measurement is the arrestment, which is a large fast speed
transient, and every result above comes from a machine turning at a constant speed.

**What this run is.** A real fault signature — Paderborn KA04, outer-race damage
from an accelerated lifetime test — replayed through a known synthetic speed
profile. The time base is warped so that `θ(τ) = ∫s dτ = f₀·t`, which preserves
every angular feature of the original (impacts, spacing, sidebands, the resonance
they excite) and changes only the speed history. Nothing about the fault is
synthesised.

**What it is not.** A measurement on genuinely variable-speed data. The Ottawa set
still has not transferred. **Do not write "we validated on variable-speed data".**

### The transient destroys conventional analysis

Real damage, 45% speed drop across the record, band frozen at 500–2000 Hz and
reused unchanged from the fixed-speed run:

| | KA04 prominence | separation vs healthy |
|---|---|---|
| Constant speed, as previously reported | 45.4 dB | **39.0 dB** |
| Swept, conventional fixed-speed analysis | 7.9 dB | **4.0 dB** |
| Swept, angle-domain resampling | 41.8 dB | **35.1 dB** |

The fault line sits at a fixed multiple of shaft speed, so when the speed sweeps
the line sweeps with it and its energy spreads across bins until it falls under the
floor. Resampling against shaft angle pins it back to a fixed bin and recovers 35 of
the 39 dB.

### Under harsher profiles, conventional analysis inverts

| profile | untracked separation | order-tracked separation |
|---|---|---|
| 45% linear | +4.2 dB | **+36.4 dB** |
| 70% linear | **−1.3 dB** | +30.3 dB |
| 70% exponential | **−0.5 dB** | +26.5 dB |
| down then up (arrestment-then-retract shape) | **−2.0 dB** | +25.2 dB |

**A negative separation means the damaged bearing reads quieter than the healthy
one.** Under a hard transient a fixed-speed monitor does not merely miss the fault —
it ranks the broken machine as the healthier of the two. That is the concrete form
of Shevach's warning, and it is worth putting in front of him in exactly those
terms.

### How accurately must the speed be known — and where AAG gets it free

Order tracking fed a deliberately wrong speed:

| speed gain error | separation |
|---|---|
| 0.0 % | 36.4 dB |
| 0.5 % | 36.2 dB |
| 1.0 % | 36.9 dB |
| **2.0 %** | **35.1 dB** |
| 3.0 % | 4.8 dB — lost |
| 5 % and above | ~0 dB — lost |

A sharp cliff between 2% and 3%, which is the peak-search window. So the
requirement is: **shaft speed known to better than about 2%.** Per-sample noise on
the speed estimate barely matters — up to 3% white noise costs nothing, because
angle is the integral of speed and the integral smooths it. What matters is bias.

**And that requirement is already met by the sensors AAG has.** The MCSA work in
§2 measured the supply fundamental at 59.99 Hz against 900 rpm and 99.97 Hz against
1500 rpm on a four-pole-pair drive — shaft speed recovered from the current channel
to better than 0.1%, roughly twenty times the margin order tracking needs, with no
tachometer.

So the answer to Mr Shevach is not "we would add an encoder". It is: the arresting
engine already carries its own speed reference in the current channel that Mr Marko
confirmed is installed, and that reference is twenty times more accurate than the
transient case requires.

### One bug caught, worth recording

The first version of this run reported ~3 dB for every condition, faulty and healthy
alike, and would have supported the conclusion that order tracking makes no
difference. The cause was a tuple unpacked backwards: `EnvelopeSpectrum.prominence_db`
returns `(order, dB)` while `mcsa.Spectrum.prominence_db` returns `(dB, order)`, and
the caller read BPFO's order number, 3.05, as a decibel figure. Two functions with
the same name and opposite return orders is a trap; they should be aligned.

The tell was that every number came out at 3.0–3.1 dB regardless of band, bearing or
condition. A result that does not move when the inputs move is not a result.
